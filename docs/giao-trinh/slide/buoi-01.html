Section 1.1

A mentor is not the best coder in the room

A mentor has two jobs. There is no third job called “write the code for them”.

① Set the requirement

Say what “done” means before the work starts, in a form that can be checked. A requirement nobody can check is a wish.

② Accept the work

Run the check yourself and read the result. Not “the team said it works”, not “the agent said it works”.

✗ Write it for them

The fastest way to reach week 6 with a team that cannot defend its own product. If you are typing in their repo, something has already gone wrong.

This is the answer to the worry in the room, and it needs to be said in the first five minutes. The building-inspector comparison is the one that lands: an inspector does not mix mortar. But an inspector knows where to knock, which paperwork must exist, and what a crack near a window means. That is exactly the skill set of these fifteen sessions. The third card is the failure mode, not a joke. Write code for a team in week 2 and in week 6 they cannot answer the judges' questions about their own system, because it is not theirs. Say that out loud; some people in the room will be tempted precisely because they want to help.

Section 1.2

The agent is the worker. You are the inspector.

Four steps. Drop step ③ and the other three become theatre.

① You

State a clear requirement

…including how it will be checked.

② Agent

Does the work

Fast, confident, and sometimes wrong.

③ You — the step people skip

Run the check

One command. Read the output with your own eyes.

④ You

Revise the requirement

The check failed? The requirement was unclear.

Using AI to write code costs no marks. Not being able to check what the AI wrote does.

This answers the team's third question — "I let the AI write most of the code, do I lose marks?" — and the answer is no. Nobody is penalised for using an agent. Marks are lost when nobody can check what the agent produced. Point at step three and say: this is the only step that cannot be delegated. Steps one, two and four can all be done with help. Step three is you, a command, and the output on screen. Every lab in all fifteen sessions ends with a check. That check is the part that is graded.

Section 2.3

At-risk triggers — your job, not the organisers'

Three mechanical signals. No judgement call required, which is exactly why they work.

2

standups missed in a row

3

days with no commit

>3

days late on a gate

When a trigger fires, open the L1 / L2 / L3 playbook in the operations course material. Do not wait for the next scheduled meeting — the point of a trigger is that it fires before anyone has formed an opinion.

These three triggers belong to the mentor, not to the organising committee. Nobody will send you a report; you watch for them. Why mechanical numbers rather than judgement: because a mentor who waits to "feel" that a team is struggling always notices in week 4, when there is no time left. Two missed standups is observable on day two. Three days with no commit is the one people argue about — "they were thinking". Fine: then there is a design document to show. No artefact for three days is the signal, whatever the reason.

Section 3.2

Three numbers to know by heart

The strongest coaching tool a mentor has — and all three cost no extra coding skill.

100%

Video demo

Missing in 11 of 11 teams. A screen recording with a voice-over. One afternoon of work.

82%

Evaluation evidence

A golden dataset and a number. Not an opinion about whether the answers “feel good”.

64%

Pitch deck

Six weeks of work, judged in eight minutes of talking. Teams discover this in week 6.

A team that delivers all three is almost certainly ahead of most of the field — without writing one line of better code.

Make the room say these three numbers out loud: one hundred, eighty-two, sixty-four. They are on the session's pass checklist, and the reason is not trivia — these are the three sentences that change a team's behaviour in week 3. Here is how to use them. When a team says "we will skip the eval to add two more features", the useful answer is not general advice about quality. It is: eighty-two percent of the previous cohort skipped it, and that is where the marks were lost. A number ends the argument; advice invites negotiation. Say the source honestly when you quote it to a team: the previous cohort of the predecessor programme. Do not inflate it, and do not present it as this cohort's data.

Say the limit out loud

Sounding certain about something uncertain is the fastest way for a mentor to lose the room's trust.

Read the middle column, not the left one. "Product completeness" means nothing to a team; "does the demo run without breaking" means something they can act on this week. Criterion two is where most teams misjudge themselves. Calling an LLM API and getting good answers is a five or six, not a nine. A nine needs retrieval over their own material, more than one guardrail layer, and a mechanism — not a prompt — that stops the model inventing things. Then say the limit honestly, because it is on the slide for a reason: the names and the level descriptions are stable, but the weights can change from cohort to cohort. At the start of each cohort you compare against the released rubric. A mentor who states a weight confidently and is wrong loses the room, and the room is the only authority a mentor has.

Section 3.4

Six engineering mistakes you can catch by opening the repo

None of these is an algorithm bug. All six are visible without reading a line of logic — which is why a mentor who does not code can still find them.

No CI/CD

Check: is there a .github/workflows/ folder, and is the latest run green?

Evaluation skipped

Check: is eval/ empty? Deliverable #10, missing in ~82% of teams.

Thin README

Check: can a stranger install and run it from the README alone?

No clear environment setup

Check: is there an .env.example and a pinned dependency list?

Everything in one file

Check: one 2000-line file, or folders that match the architecture diagram?

No tests

Check: does tests/ exist, and what does make test actually print?

Source: docs/giao-trinh/00-khung-danh-gia.md §5

These are trade mistakes, not algorithm mistakes, and that distinction is the point of the slide. You do not need to understand a team's retrieval logic to see that their eval folder is empty. Read the check line on each card, because the check is the deliverable of this slide. Six cards, six commands or six clicks. That is a repo review, and it takes about ten minutes. The last one has a trap in it that we will use in the lab today: do not ask whether tests exist, run them and read the output. "We have tests" and "the tests pass" are different statements, and teams routinely confuse them.

Section 4.1

MT-01 — an AI agent for standardising teaching quality

Case: TTN – UEH. One project, three layers. The one project every session of this course is built on.

Layer A

The learner

Grounded question answering over approved material, plus support for students studying in a second language.

Reads only what layer B has approved.

Layer B

The course

Syllabus review against a 6-criterion rubric, with a lecturer who approves or rejects. The human stays in the loop.

This is the layer people mistake for the whole project.

Layer C

The programme

Anonymised, aggregated numbers across courses — so a programme manager can see the shape of the whole curriculum.

Its data comes from approved reviews in B.

Source: docs/de-bai.md — the project brief, in your repo

Introduce the project, and immediately correct the misreading that happens every time: MT-01 is not "a syllabus checker". That is layer B, the middle one. The three layers are chained, and the chain is the design. Only material approved in B becomes a source for A, and only reviews completed in B produce numbers for C. If someone proposes to build A first with unapproved documents, they have broken the chain and the product no longer means anything. Say what we actually do with it: we build it for real, deploy it for real, evaluate it for real. By the end of the fifteen sessions each mentor owns a repo that has been through G1 to G6 — which is the only honest way to mentor a team through those gates.

Section 4.2

The five-step flow of layer B

Where the human sits in the loop is the whole design. Step ④ is not optional, and it is not a formality.

1

A lecturer submits a syllabus

PDF or DOCX, with the course code and term. Nothing happens automatically before this.

2

The agent reviews it against the rubric

Six criteria. Every finding must cite the passage it came from — a finding with no citation is a hallucination with good manners.

3

A draft report is produced

Status PENDING. Visible to the lecturer, invisible to students.

4

The lecturer approves or rejects — the human in the loop

Only an approved report becomes source material for layer A and a data point for layer C. The agent never approves its own work.

5

Approved content flows outward

To layer A as a citable source, to layer C as an anonymised number.

Source: docs/de-bai.md §Ba năng lực

Walk down the five steps and stop at step four. Step four is the reason this project is worth building. An agent that reviewed syllabi and published the results itself would be unusable in a real university, and would also be a much easier piece of software. The approval gate is what makes it real, and it is also what makes it technically interesting: a pending state, a permission check, and a flow that waits for a person. Note the sentence in step two for later use in session 07: a finding without a citation is a hallucination with good manners. That is the guardrail requirement for G3, expressed in a form a team can test.

Section 4.3

Three roles, and the boundary between them

“Who is allowed to do this?” is a server-side question. A UI that hides a button has not answered it.

Student

Can

  • ask questions, get cited answers
  • read approved material only
  • ask for ESL support on a passage

Must never see a PENDING report.

Lecturer

Can the approval

  • submit a syllabus
  • read the draft report
  • approve or reject — their own courses

Approving someone else's course → 403, from the server.

Programme manager

Can

  • see anonymised aggregates
  • compare across courses and terms
  • export the programme view

Cannot read one named lecturer's report.

The check for this, from session 03 onward: call the approve endpoint as the wrong role and confirm the server returns 403. Hiding the button in the UI is not a permission system.

Three roles, and one sentence that matters: permissions live on the server. Teams will implement this in the front end first, because it is easier and it looks right in a demo. Then a judge opens the network tab, calls the endpoint directly, and the whole permission model evaporates. We will test exactly this in session 03's exercise, and it is worth saying today so nobody is surprised. The manager's restriction is the subtle one. Aggregated and anonymised means the manager cannot drill down to a named lecturer, even though the data exists. That is a design decision from the brief, not an oversight, and a team will try to "improve" it in week 3.

Scope guard — what MT-01 deliberately does not do

Read this list aloud and put it on the wall. You will need it in week 3, word for word.

✗

✗

No grading of students

✗

No multi-agent setup

✗

No fine-tuning

✗

No Next.js in the basic version

In week 3 a team will want to add a feature instead of finishing the one they have. This list is the answer, and it is easier to give when it was written down in week 1.

Read the five items out loud. They sound like limitations; they are the project's spine. Explain why each one is excluded, briefly: a real LMS integration is someone else's credentials and someone else's release cycle; grading students is an ethics review, not a sprint; multi-agent and fine-tuning are both ways to spend six weeks and have nothing to demo; Next.js is a front-end decision the brief defers so the team can ship something visible in week 1. Then the real point: scope creep in week 3 is the single most common way a team fails Demo Day, and it never arrives as "let us abandon the plan". It arrives as one more good idea. A mentor who agreed the scope in writing in week 1 can say no in one sentence in week 3.

Section 5 · the most important slide today

The agent is most confident about what it never checked

Ask it two questions about your own repo. Then run the commands yourself.

Question 1 — what the agent says

“How many files does this repo track?”

A confident number. Usually wrong.

Question 2 — what the agent says

“What happens if I run make test?”

“The tests pass.” Said with total certainty.

Why it is wrong

Not because it is weak. Because it guessed instead of running — and it guesses towards the most familiar world, where every repo's tests are green.

The truth — run it

$ git ls-files | wc -l
104

$ make test
...
2 passed, 3 errors

The three errors are left red on purpose. Session 02 fixes them, and the fixing is the lesson.

The rule of this whole course

A question about the state of a repo is answered by a command — never by a sentence.

This is the slide to spend the most time on, and the one the lab turns into a habit. Run both commands live if you can. The file count moves as the course material grows, so check it before the session with git ls-files piped to wc -l and use today's number. The test result is the interesting one: two passed, three errors, and those errors are deliberate — the template ships with a real defect so that session 02 has something real to fix. Say the diagnosis carefully, because it changes how people treat the tool for the rest of the course. The agent is not failing at arithmetic. It answered from the most probable world rather than from this repo, and in the most probable world the tests are green. That failure mode never announces itself; it arrives as a fluent, plausible sentence. Then give the rule, and mean it: questions about repo state are answered by a command. Everybody will hear this sentence from their own team within three weeks, phrased as "it works on my machine".

Lab · 60 minutes · four exercises

Four people, one team, one repo, four pull requests

The hard part of GitHub is not typing the commands. It is four people editing one repo without overwriting each other.

The six Git moves — all the GitHub you need for fifteen sessions

MoveWhat it actually means
git switch -c <branch>Open your own draft. Nobody else sees it; nobody gets overwritten.
git add <file>Choose exactly what you are submitting — not everything you touched.
git commit -m "…"Package it, with the reason. Someone will read that sentence, including you in three weeks.
git push -u origin <branch>Send the draft to GitHub. Still not in the main branch.
gh pr createAsk: “this is what I want to add.” It opens a place for someone to read it.
gh pr mergeAgree, and take it in. The reviewer's job, not the author's.

The team's law, from today to the end of the course

Nobody merges their own pull request.

Not red tape. This is the mentor's whole trade compressed into one action: somebody else reads the work before it counts as done.

Haven't accepted the collaborator invitation yet? Fix it now, not at exercise 1.2.

Show this immediately before the lab starts, and say the framing sentence first: this whole cohort is one team on one repo. Not one repo each. Their real teams will work this way, and the difficulty of GitHub was never the commands. Read the "what it actually means" column out loud before anyone types. Six moves, six plain sentences. The one people get wrong is the last: merging is the reviewer's action. Authors push, reviewers merge. Then state the law as a law, not a procedure. Nobody merges their own PR. The reason to say it today, on a one-line change to a README, is that a habit formed on a trivial change survives into week 4 when the change is frightening. Last thing before you release the room: anyone whose collaborator invitation is still sitting in their GitHub inbox should fix it right now. Otherwise they will discover it in the middle of exercise 1.2 with a push that fails.

More than one line changed means you touched somebody else's row, or git add -A pulled in something extra. That is the most common week-1 mistake on a real team.

This is the core exercise of the session: the first pull request most people in the room have ever opened. The design is deliberate. Everyone gets their own row, so four pull requests into the same table merge cleanly and nobody meets a merge conflict on their first day. Conflicts are a later session's lesson, and meeting one now would teach fear rather than process. Spend a moment on the output check, because it is the transferable skill. One plus line, one minus line. A diff that is bigger than the change you described is the single most useful warning sign a reviewer has, and it is why a mentor opens the Files changed tab before approving anything. And hold the line on the last column. A question written to look good is worthless; session 03 is built from these questions, so a vague one wastes the writer's own time.

Exercise 1.3 · 12 minutes

Review the pull request next to you — then merge it

① Task

Take the pull request of the person beside you. Check three things, leave one real question, then approve and merge.

Afterwards, bring the team's work down to your own machine — and notice that it did not arrive by itself.

This is the mentor's trade, compressed into one action: somebody else reads the work before it counts as done.

Twelve minutes, and the law is the whole content: nobody merges their own pull request. Say why it is a law and not a procedure. The three checks on this slide are a real code review shrunk to fit a one-line change — scope, truthfulness, substance. Those are the same three questions you will ask in week 4 about a change to the agent's guardrail, where you cannot read the logic. The habit is what transfers, not the checks themselves. The question before the approval is not a formality either. It forces the reviewer to have read the content rather than just the diff size. Then the last two commands, which teach the thing teams get wrong for six weeks: merging happens on GitHub, and your machine knows nothing about it until you pull. "Why don't I have that file" in week 3 is almost always this.

  Ran it: 2 passed, 3 errors.

Four parts, every time: what it claimed · what is true · why it was wrong · the command that settles it. The “why” is the part that makes it reusable.

Eight minutes, and it is the exercise that carries the session's one transferable idea. Insist on one question per turn. Asked together, the agent often hedges on both; asked separately, you get the confident wrong answer that makes the point. The journal line has four parts and the third is the one people drop. What it claimed and what is true are easy. Why it was wrong is what turns an anecdote into something you can recognise again — and recognising it in your own team's standup is the actual skill. The grey box matters more every month: careful agents do run the command first. That is not a failed exercise, it is a better one. Ask which command, run it yourself, compare. A mentor's question is "what did you run", not "are you sure".
